A synchronous, always-visible text channel that gives someone who cannot speak the same presence in a call as someone talking. Shipped across four SDKs ahead of a European accessibility deadline, and ahead of Teams.

The design problem was never the text field. It was a platform with no way to signal the feature was on, two design reversals I made when better evidence appeared, and two moments where I refused to let a reasonable business request take away a deaf user's equal footing in a call.

RTT typing

§01Problem

A call where the loudest voice is a keyboard.

Picture a video call where one person cannot speak. Maybe they are deaf or hard of hearing, maybe they have a speech disability, maybe they are somewhere they cannot safely talk out loud. In most calling products that person drops to second class. They get a chat box off to the side that everyone else can collapse and forget, and their words wait in a queue while the conversation moves on without them.

Real-Time Text is the feature that fixes that. As you type, the text appears immediately, letter by letter, in the main call stage, so a typed contribution carries the same weight as a spoken one. The conversation feels live, closer to talking than to messaging.

The forcing function

RTT was not a nice-to-have. Under the European Accessibility Act (Directive 2019/882), real-time text became a compliance requirement for voice and video platforms in the EU as of June 30, 2025. Put plainly, a service without it could not operate in Europe or serve European customers past that date. We had to ship, and we had to ship before our own video calling story was complete.

The deadline explains why the work existed. The design problem itself started with who the feature is for. RTT matters for people who are deaf or hard of hearing, for neurodiverse users, and for anyone with a speech disability. It lets them communicate in real time, with hearing users and with each other, without needing an interpreter in the room. It also matters in emergencies, where speaking out loud may be impossible or unsafe. Once you frame the user that way, the design goal writes itself: text has to have equal footing with speech. If someone is typing, they are participating as fully as someone talking, and the interface has to treat them that way.

That principle became the thing every hard decision got measured against.

§02Process

What I got wrong first.

The first version of my design got two things wrong, and both are worth showing, because fixing them is where the design actually happened.

I originally proposed a fifteen-second editable window, the span of time a typist could still revise text before it locked in. I at least had the honesty to say we should make that duration easy to change, since I did not know whether ten, fifteen, or thirty seconds was right. It turned out to be none of them. Looking at how RTT already worked on iOS and Samsung devices, the established pattern was two to three seconds before text commits. Fifteen felt sluggish and strange next to that. I moved it to three, matching the muscle memory people already had.

I also had RTT replacing live captions outright, on the assumption that two streams of text on screen would compete. That was backwards. Captions and RTT are different things doing different jobs, and users needed both. So I reworked it. The captions surface became the shell that hosts RTT, a single window for both. When RTT is on, captions can still be toggled alongside it.

§03Constraint

One missing signal shaped everything.

Partway through, engineering surfaced a platform limitation that reshaped the whole design. There was no explicit signal to tell a client that RTT was active on a call. The client had to infer it, by watching for incoming real-time text data and reasoning backward.

That one missing signal cascaded into three decisions a user can actually see. The RTT panel cannot be closed once it opens, because if we let people dismiss it, someone typing could go unseen, which defeats the entire purpose. RTT cannot be turned on before a call connects, because we had no way to know it was active until the call was live and data was flowing, so pre-join enablement had to go. And message attribution had to be handled in the client itself, mapping text back to the right participant, because the layer below could not hand us that mapping.

I like this part because it is the reality of platform design. The version I first drew needed a signal the platform did not send, so the real work became shaping that constraint into something that felt deliberate.

§04Decisions

Two decisions I defended, and one I reversed.

The first was whether the RTT panel should be allowed to appear on its own when someone starts typing. A teammate pointed out, fairly, that a panel sliding in mid-call is jarring and can push the call controls out from under someone reaching for mute. The proposed fix was a subtle indicator instead. I decided against it. A subtle indicator is exactly the second-class treatment RTT exists to remove. If someone is typing, their words have to show, even at the cost of a small jolt to everyone else. Accessibility wins the tie.

The second was whether developers should be able to switch RTT off entirely. The argument for it was commercial and completely understandable: a customer with no EU users might not want the feature at all. I said no. An accessibility feature you can quietly disable stops being an accessibility feature. The moment a business can decide a deaf user does not get equal footing, the guarantee is gone.

Not every call went my way. I had originally specced the ability to mute an RTT participant, by direct analogy to muting a speaker. A colleague challenged it on dignity grounds. Muting someone's typed voice strips their controls and sends a strange message, and they were right. It got scaled back to an organizer-only capability rather than something anyone could do to anyone.

§05Scope

What I cut, and why.

Shipping on a legal deadline means being ruthless about scope. The v1 spec promised RTT everywhere; the shipped table said no to a lot of it. Here is what I dropped, and why.

Translation
Nobody could settle what translating character-by-character text even means as it streams in, and the technical path we chose closed off server-side processing anyway. Captions can be translated; RTT, for now, cannot. A real gap I chose to accept rather than hold the ship.
PSTN
Bringing RTT to phone calls depends on carriers deciding how they will implement and deploy it. We understood the shape of the problem, but you cannot ship against partners who have not made their decision yet.
Teams interop
RTT in Teams interop scenarios would follow once Teams itself implemented it, so we scoped it out of the first release rather than build against a moving target.
Pre-join
Cut for the platform-signal reason above. We could not know RTT was on until the call connected.

§06Solution

What shipped.

RTT went out as four components in the UI Library, built to drop into the calling composites.

RealTimeText
Shows a single line as it is typed, with an is-typing indicator.
RealTimeTextModal
Tells everyone RTT is now on and cannot be turned off.
StartRealTimeTextButton
Sits in the call control bar.
CaptionsBanner
Renders captions and RTT in one window, tags in-progress typing, folds committed messages into the feed.

The interaction model is simple by design. RTT is off by default. Any participant can start it from the control bar. Starting it turns it on for everyone, and once on, it stays on for the rest of the call. A message commits when you press Enter or after three seconds without typing, and stays editable until then. It shipped in UI Library version 1.24.0 and later.

The clearest way I found to explain RTT is by contrast with chat. Chat is asynchronous. It can be collapsed or hidden, which gives it a secondary place in the call. RTT is synchronous. It shows text as it is typed, in the main gallery, and the window cannot be hidden or closed. That is what gives it the same level of prominence as speaking.

Underneath, the work sat on real standards. RTT transports real-time text over RTP following RFC 4103, and the accessibility standard, EN 301 549 section 6.2, sets a hard latency target: typed text has to reach the network within 500 milliseconds of a reliably composed unit, and under 300 milliseconds to feel fluid. Most of this design lived in timing, and it was a fun problem to build against.

§07Outcome

Shipped on schedule, across four SDKs, ahead of Teams.

Public preview landed for the Native Calling SDK first, then on January 30, 2025 for the Web Calling SDK, Web UI Library, and Native UI Library, followed by general availability in Calling Web SDK 1.34.1 and UI Library 1.24.0. It shipped ahead of the June 2025 deadline, and ahead of Teams, which gave customers the maximum runway to implement it in their own apps.

I drove the full launch: the rollout across all four SDKs, plus the announcements, demos, and workshops around it. None of it was solo work. RTT existed because of the collaboration between the Calling Web, Native, UI, and Design teams, and our partners on IC3 and Teams.

§08Hindsight

What I would do differently.

I shipped a compliance-critical accessibility feature without the instrumentation to prove anyone used it. I flagged the telemetry gap early, and I knew exactly what I wanted to measure, messages sent and received, session duration, adoption by region, but the numbers were not there when it counted. If I did it again, the telemetry would be part of the ship, not a follow-up.

There was a screen-reader question I raised and never fully closed: how a screen-reader user is notified that RTT messages are arriving. It was on my list, and it did not get resolved in the release. I would rather name that gap than pretend it was closed.

And I committed to full call-type coverage before I understood what the platform could actually signal. Every one of those early commitments, Teams interop, PSTN, pre-join, came back out. The lesson I took is to map the platform's real capabilities before writing the scope table, not after.
