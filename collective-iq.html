Collective IQ

Designing how organizations remember.

Overview

An AI memory for sales teams, built on one deliberate act: choosing to contribute.

Copilot's intelligence is bounded by individual participation.

Picture an eight-person account team. One person ran the kickoff, someone else took the last call, a third has been going back and forth with procurement over email. Each of them is holding a piece of the customer relationship, and none of it reaches the rest of the team. Collective IQ pulls that scattered context out of people's meetings and hands it to Copilot as grounding it can actually trust.

On paper this is a sharing feature: a button that pushes a meeting summary somewhere other people can use it. The button was never the hard part. The hard part was getting someone to press it, to believe that handing their notes to the wider org wouldn't come back on them later, while the org stayed able to stand behind whatever Copilot surfaced from it. Nobody contributes if that trust isn't there.

What I owned

I set the interaction model end to end, from a loose strategic direction to the contributor experience that shipped.

Six full design approaches across different surfaces and modalities; the one that went to MVP was mine, and the others are feeding phases 2 and 3. The work spanned what people see before a meeting, how they share afterward, the receipts they get back, the governance layer, and the surfaces that later flag what the org still doesn't know.

Context

Gong and Glean proved companies pay for this. The context already existed, just locked inside individual histories.

From individual meeting rooms to shared organizational memory.

We weren't guessing that this market exists. Gong and Glean had already shown that companies will pay for AI that knows what the whole organization knows, not only one person's slice of it. Inside Microsoft the same gap was sitting in plain sight. The useful customer context lived in each person's private data, walled off from the rest of their account team, even in the cases where sharing it would obviously help the next conversation.

We started with sales because the customer-brief moment makes the gap impossible to miss. Before a meeting that matters, the team needs to know things that live in other people's calendars: who else has talked to this account, what got promised, what's still open. The answers exist. They're just trapped in individual meeting histories, which is what CIQ goes after.

Each touchpoint does a different job:

  • A meeting is where the context gets made, just by people talking
  • The Teams feed is where a summary shows up and asks if you want to contribute it
  • Sharing is where you set who gets to see it
  • Copilot is where someone pulls it back out later, in a different workflow entirely

There's a new step in here, sitting between a conversation and anything the org can use from it. Someone has to deliberately contribute it.

Problem

The whole design is one balance: keep contributors in control without making the system useless.

Communication is sensitive by default.

A real conversation is full of half-formed ideas, things people aren't sure about yet, the give-and-take of a negotiation. Push that out to a wider audience and you've made something risky, especially with customers, where someone might act on it.

The hardest part was the balance. Give people too many knobs and they don't bother contributing; give them too few and they stop trusting the system with anything real.

It also couldn't live in one place. The same knowledge gets made in a meeting, shown in a feed, then pulled up much later somewhere else entirely, like a search result or a Copilot answer.

Why this was hard

Designing the interaction model on top of a product still being argued out, with legal in the room throughout.

The product definition was still evolving as I was designing it.

What goes in the MVP, who owns the sharing action, how topics and categories should work: all of it was still being argued out while I worked. I was designing the interaction model on top of a product definition that hadn't settled yet.

Compliance wasn't a final-stage review. I was working with legal the whole way through, and they shaped real design decisions: the wording on a card, which states it could be in, how the data model was structured, when the consent prompt fired. Those were legal calls as much as UX ones.

The brief asked for two things at once: make it work for Teams meetings today, and don't paint yourself into a corner, because this eventually has to cover email, channels, and chats too. So every structural call had to hold up at both sizes. The contributor/beneficiary model, the way sharing is scoped to a single summary, the governance layer: none of them could be so tuned to meetings that they'd break when the surface changed.

Design

One screen does the heavy lifting: the point where you choose to share or not.

A post-meeting decision moment.

This is the moment that decides whether something private becomes something the org can use.

Take this step away and the system either leaks everything or nobody touches it. Trust holds or falls apart right here.

System

No settings page. The control is the choice itself: everyone, a few people, or no one.

Control happens in the moment you share.

Decisions

The calls that cost the most, on what to expose, how much friction, and how much to build.

Three decisions that shaped the MVP.

Summaries instead of transcripts

A full transcript shows everything: the hedging, the half-thoughts, the part of a negotiation nobody wants quoted back at them. Share that and people stop contributing.

A summary keeps the useful part and drops most of the exposure. That's the trade that made anyone willing to share at all.

Friction for trust

We could have shared everything automatically and gotten far more coverage. It also would have taken the decision out of people's hands, and that kind of trust goes fast.

So there's a manual review step. A little friction, on purpose. People stay in control of their own material, which is the only reason they'll take part at all.

Simplification over flexibility

Every extra role, category, and review screen we added made the system more flexible and more confusing at the same time, and the confusion is what stopped people from contributing.

Stripping it back did more than piling it on ever did. Fewer states, one obvious primary action, a scope model you could reason about without thinking hard. The simpler it got, the more people used it.

States

The unglamorous part: a summary that fails, a share that fails, content that expires.

What it looks like when things go wrong.

Impact

The payoff, in practice: walking into a meeting already knowing conversations you were never part of.

Getting an org to share what it knows.

Before CIQ, whatever got said in a meeting stayed with whoever was in it. A team's customer context sat in scattered individual histories and never got to the people who actually needed it.

The contribution model gave people a way to pass along the gist of a meeting without handing over the sensitive parts. That's the foundation Copilot needs to answer from what the whole org knows, not just whatever the person asking happens to have access to.

In practice, a rep could walk into a customer meeting already knowing how earlier conversations went, including ones they were never in. The knowledge stopped being stuck with whoever happened to be in the room.
