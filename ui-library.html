The ACS UI Library is an open-source SDK of prebuilt voice, video, and chat experiences, built on top of the Azure Communication Services Calling, Chat, and Rooms SDKs. It ships in two tiers: composites that drop a whole calling or chat experience into a product in a few lines, and components a developer assembles into their own. Both run on web, iOS, and Android. It carries 11.1M+ monthly minutes and ships inside 200+ businesses.

I'm the product lead and the designer. I set the interaction model, made the visual calls, and wrote the specs. I also own the roadmap and the customer conversations for those same features. 40+ have shipped with a team of 20+ engineers.

Everything here is designed through defaults. Developers adopt the library; their patients, customers, and agents just get whatever we shipped, without ever choosing it. So one control bar has to hold up in a telehealth appointment and in a contact center, and still bend far enough that a team can make it theirs.

§01Problem

A component library only matters if developers actually reach for it.

The UI Library gives developers turn-key voice, video, and chat UI so they can add communication to their apps without building it from scratch. The promise is speed: a working, accessible call experience in days instead of months, themeable to a brand.

For much of the time I owned it, the honest story was not the founding vision. It was adoption. Developers were not reaching for the library as often as that promise suggested they would, so I set out to learn why rather than assume. What I found reframed the whole roadmap.

The stakes ran three ways. Developers were spending months building this themselves. Their end users needed the accessibility features that are hardest for a small team to get right. And the business cared because every app built on the library is an app running on the platform.

§02Role

I owned the library as a product, and that meant far more than the screens.

I ran the research that told us where it really stood, rewrote the strategy around what we learned, designed and shipped features across the system, and drove the accessibility work against a legal deadline.

I wrote the specs in a press-release-first format. Every feature began with the sentence a customer would eventually read, so the team designed backward from that outcome, starting from the result a customer wanted.

None of it was solo work. I worked in close partnership with engineering and design throughout, and the founding vision and telemetry belonged to others on the team. The calls described here are the ones I made or drove.

§03System

One system, many levels of control.

The library is built in two tiers. Composites are turn-key experiences for common scenarios, packaged to drop straight into an app. Components are the building blocks beneath them, for teams that need custom layouts or their own behavior. Accessibility and localization run across both, shown in Fig. 01 above as a ladder: the more you want to shape the experience, the lower you reach, and the more you take on.

Three axes decide where a team lands: identity, meaning bring your own or use Microsoft 365 identity to reach Teams resources; platform, meaning web and native; and look and feel. The headline I wrote for it: build the exact user experience you want.

Theming runs on Fluent. Documentation was itself a design decision. Component docs need to be visual and interactive, so we built on Storybook, where a developer can try a component with their own IDs before writing a line of code.

Over its life the system shipped a broad feature set:

  • Background blur & replacement
  • Closed captions
  • Reactions
  • Breakout rooms
  • Together mode
  • PowerPoint Live
  • Custom & pinned layouts
  • Spotlight
  • Noise suppression
  • End-of-call survey
  • E911
  • Real-time text ↗

§04Findings

The obvious roadmap was the wrong one.

The instinct was to chase feature parity with Teams. The research said something else. Customers wanted control over branding and customization more than they wanted the next feature, and several had turned away because the library felt less malleable than we had promised. I ranked customization first, ahead of parity, and made that the strategy.

The sharpest finding was about how people reached the library in the first place. It was hard to discover, and you could click through page after page of docs before you ever got to it. An adoption problem that looked like a product-quality problem was, in large part, a problem of getting people to the front door. An independent developer starting cold hit the same wall the interviewed customers described. Same problem, both ends.

The assumption I had to drop was that low-code versus pro-code was the axis that mattered. It wasn't. Customers were fine with that balance. What they wanted was their brand, built their way.

§05Outcome

It found its audience and grew fast.

One of ACS's most-used surfaces, shipping in healthcare, finance, automotive, retail, and enterprise products. Every composite meets WCAG 2.1 AA before a developer touches it.

As the platform evolved, this stayed one of the clearest examples in ACS of a system that met developers where they were: reach for a composite when you want speed, drop to components when you want control. By the time it matured, developers were using it to add voice, video, and chat to their apps in days, with accessibility handled by default.

Two of the smaller decisions underneath it: in-progress text is dimmed rather than colored, and turning RTT on asks for consent before it starts.
